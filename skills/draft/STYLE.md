# The sniff test

One rule above all the others: **would a reader assume a machine wrote
this?** If yes, nothing else about the post matters. Readers have got good
at spotting AI social posts, so failing this test does more damage than a
weak idea does.

Every skill in this suite reads this file. The suite's `../tells/TELLS.md`
enforces it.

## Write plain English statements, freely

The reference is your own best plain-English writing: how you would
explain the thing to a person sitting across from you, not a page built to
be quotable. Full sentences that explain a thing and land on what it means
for the reader. Nothing compressed into a shape. Nothing arranged to sound
quotable.

## The four shapes that give it away

**Aphorisms.** Lines built to be screenshotted. If a sentence sounds like
it wants to be a quote card, it is wrong. Say the thing plainly instead.

**This, but that.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Not a tool. A system." Every
variant of the reversal. It is the single most overused construction in AI
social copy and readers now register it instantly.

**The pithy fragment.** A short word or phrase, a comma or full stop, then
a clipped ending for emphasis. "Simple, right?" "Every time." "That's it."
"Not close." Machines reach for this because it feels punchy. It reads as
performed.

**Stacked fragments.** "Fast. Simple. Done." Three beats where a sentence
would do.

## Fewer and simpler words, not shorter posts

This is not about length. A long post is fine. It is about whether the
same thing could be said with plainer words and fewer of them. If it
could, do that.

Cut: intensifiers doing no work, throat-clearing before the point,
restating the opening at the close, any adjective that is not carrying a
fact.

## Contract your words

"Wouldn't", not "would not". "It's", not "it is". "Doesn't", "won't",
"here's", "that's". Formal full forms in casual writing are a tell,
because the register does not match the room.

## Casual over correct

Aim for how you would say it out loud, not for a clean sentence. Slightly
loose is better than slightly stiff.

Spelling stays consistent with whatever your own house style is; what
loosens is the formality, not the dictionary.

## The imperfection rule, and its trap

A machine never leaves a rough edge. Occasionally starting a sentence
without a capital, or letting a sentence run on a bit, or ending somewhere
slightly untidy, reads as a person typing.

**The trap: a quota turns this into a pattern.** "Add one lowercase
opening per post" is itself a machine behaviour, and a detectable one. The
moment the roughness is regular it is worse than no roughness, and you
have built a new tell while trying to remove one.

So the rule is subtractive, not additive. **Do not sand it off.** Write it
the way you would actually say it and leave what falls out. If nothing
rough falls out, leave it smooth. Never manufacture an error to hit a
target.

And never a real mistake. Not a wrong word, not a broken sentence, not a
misspelling, and never anything wrong in a number or a name. Careless in
the typing, never careless in the facts.

## Learnings beat opinions

The most important rule in this file, and the one that decides what you
write about at all.

An opinion has to be earned. Years of doing the work buy the right to say
"here is what I think", and a reader will weigh the opinion against who is
saying it. Most people do not yet have an established byline, and
pretending otherwise reads badly.

A learning needs none of it. "We measured this and here is what came back"
is judged on the measurement, not on who took it. Data travels without a
reputation attached, and it is judged less harshly, because there is
nothing to argue with except the method.

If you have something that lets you measure rather than opine, that is the
asset: use it. If you do not yet, borrow other people's measurements,
cited, until you do. So the default posture is **here is what we found**,
not here is what I think.

The practical consequence: when something interesting turns up, the first
question is not "what is my take on this" but **"what could I measure that
would settle it"**. Then run it, then post the finding. That path is
slower and it produces something nobody else can copy, because nobody else
ran your measurement.

Opinions are still allowed, but they earn their place by sitting on top of
a measurement rather than replacing one.

## Posture

Decide your posture once, and write it down, rather than reinventing it
every post. Two decisions matter most:

**Do you show the work as you build, or only publish findings once they
land?** Both are legitimate postures with real audiences. What tends to go
wrong is drifting between them: a feed that is half progress updates and
half findings reads as unplanned. Pick one, or a deliberate ratio, and
stay inside it.

**Do you show weakness, or only confidence?** A little of the real person
is what makes a post readable. Struggling, scrappy, or asking for
encouragement, reads well to some audiences and badly to others; know
which one you are writing for. If your audience is buying confidence from
you, undercutting it in the same breath does more harm than the honesty
earns you.

**Useful to your actual reader, first and last.** What has always worked
still works. Share something they can actually use. Everything else is
decoration.

## The read-aloud test

Read the post out loud. Two failures to listen for:

Every sentence the same length, so nothing makes you stop. Machine text is
rhythmically even; a person's is lumpy.

Anything you would not actually say to someone. If you would not say it in
a pub, do not post it.

## Provenance

Every published fact carries its footnote, and the footnote works.

- A number, a date, a study, a claim about the world: give it a numbered
  source, and make sure the source has a working link to the primary,
  publishing both together. A citation mark with nothing behind it reads
  as opinion dressed up as evidence.
- This is positioning, not decoration: being checkable is what marks out
  someone who actually knows the domain, and it is also what makes a page
  worth quoting back by an answer engine rather than merely present in the
  results.
- A quiet presentation works best: idle, a citation is a small superscript
  that does not catch a scanning eye; on hover or focus it gains contrast;
  the click target is generous even though the mark is small.
- Applies to any long-form piece with facts in it. Short-form posts can
  carry the link to the fuller piece; the fuller piece carries the
  provenance.
- **Going back to fix things preserves the original date.** Fixing a typo,
  adding a citation, swapping a byline: none of that should touch the date
  printed on the piece. A page that claims to have gone live today every
  time you touch it is telling a small lie about the work, and it reads as
  one. Reserve a date change for an actual substantive revision a reader
  would care about (new findings, a reversed conclusion, a rewritten
  section).
